    How it works¶

    Three cities, one engine¶

    Nothing in the engine is London-specific. ridegraph/cities.py gives each city its area, the places a person would name on the map, where ride demand concentrates and which side of the road traffic drives on; the same pipeline downloads, builds, contracts, trains and simulates each one, and the map switches between them, keeping the view inside the chosen city.

    City Area Extras
    Central London Paddington to Whitechapel, King's Cross to Vauxhall Congestion Charge, live TfL closures, checked against OSRM and published speeds
    Nicosia The south of the city, stopping short of the buffer zone so no route crosses the Green Line Drives on the left, like London
    Manhattan Battery to Central Park South, with the bridges to Brooklyn and Queens Drives on the right, so turn costs are mirrored

    The time-of-day traffic pattern is London's everywhere; only London's has been checked against real figures.

    1. The map is a graph¶

    Intersections become nodes and road segments become directed edges. A two-way street is two edges; a one-way street or roundabout is one.

    The pipeline (ridegraph/osm.py) pulls drivable ways from OpenStreetMap, drops what a car cannot use (footpaths, private roads, car park aisles), applies oneway, roundabout and motorway direction rules, then keeps only the largest strongly connected component. That last step matters: cutting a city out of a bounding box leaves stubs you can drive into but never leave, and a router that starts there would fail.

    2. Edges cost seconds, not metres¶

    The quickest route is not the shortest one. Each edge's weight is its travel time (ridegraph/speeds.py):

    seconds = length / free-flow speed

    Free-flow speed comes from the posted maxspeed when OSM has it, discounted to what drivers actually sustain between junctions, or from a typical speed for the road class otherwise. So 900 m of main road can beat 600 m of back street, and the tests check that it does.

    The raw map (RoadNetwork) and the weighted routing graph (Graph) are kept separate, so swapping in a traffic model means rebuilding weights, not re-downloading the map. Edges are stored in CSR (compressed sparse row) form, the layout production engines use: all edges leaving node u sit contiguously in head[first_out[u]:first_out[u + 1]], with a mirrored incoming index for searches that run backwards.

    3. Four ways to search¶

    Dijkstra (dijkstra.py) grows a ball of settled nodes outward from the start in order of travel time; when the destination is settled, its time is provably optimal. It is the reference every other algorithm must agree with, and its weakness motivates the rest: the ball expands in every direction, including away from the destination.

    Bidirectional Dijkstra (bidirectional.py) searches from both ends at once, so two half-size balls replace one big one. The subtle part is the stopping rule: the first node both searches reach is not necessarily on the best route, so the search tracks the best joined route seen and stops only once the two frontiers together cannot beat it.

    A* (astar.py) adds a sense of direction by ranking nodes on time so far plus an estimate of time remaining: straight-line distance at the network's top speed. That estimate can never overshoot, so results stay optimal while the search stretches towards the target. In a city the gain is bounded because "top speed in a straight line" is very optimistic.

    Contraction Hierarchies (ch.py) is what production routing engines rely on. A one-off preprocessing step ranks nodes by importance and removes them from least to most important. Whenever removing node v would break a quickest route u -> v -> w, a shortcut edge u -> w is added, unless a local witness search finds another route that is just as quick.

    Afterwards, every quickest route can be found by searching only upwards in the ranking from both ends, so each query explores a small cone instead of a ball of the city. The implementation includes lazy priority updates during ordering, bounded witness searches, stall-on-demand at query time, and recursive unpacking of shortcuts back into real road segments.

    4. Traffic and turns¶

    Rush hour (traffic.py). The same road has a different speed at 3am and 8am. Each edge's speed is scaled by a city-wide congestion curve for the hour (separate weekday and weekend curves) and by how sensitive that class of road is to congestion: arterials lose up to half their speed at the peak, residential streets far less, because through-traffic avoids them. So at 8am the quickest route can leave the main road for back streets, and the tests check that it does. The curves are illustrative, shaped after London's familiar pattern rather than fitted to measured data; production systems use historical and live per-segment speeds from vehicle GPS traces, which would slot in here without touching the routing code.

    Turns (turns.py). In a plain road graph, a node is an intersection, so the search cannot tell turning right across oncoming traffic from carrying straight on. The fix is an edge-expanded graph: each node is a directed road segment ("I just drove u to v") and each edge is a manoeuvre ("then take v to w"), costing the next segment's travel time plus a turn penalty. UK defaults make right turns (crossing traffic) dearer than left turns, with extra cost for sharp turns, U-turns and traffic lights parsed from OSM. A bend in a single road is not a junction, so it costs nothing.

    Every algorithm runs on the expanded graph unchanged, Contraction Hierarchies included, since it is just another weighted graph. A Router (router.py) builds the right graph for a scenario and maps routes back to intersections.

    $ python -m ridegraph eta <from> <to> --turns
03:00 weekday, with turn costs    2 min 20 s   1.01 km  reference route
08:00 weekday, with turn costs    3 min 59 s   0.99 km  different route (30% shared)
13:00 weekday, with turn costs    3 min 06 s   1.01 km  same route
17:30 weekday, with turn costs    3 min 50 s   0.99 km  different route (30% shared)

    Synthetic street grid; London output lands with the next data refresh.

    5. Live closures and Customizable Contraction Hierarchies¶

    Contraction Hierarchies are fast because they are built for one set of travel times. A closure on Oxford Street makes them stale, and rebuilding takes far longer than a user will wait. Production engines (OSRM's MLD, Bing Maps) separate the road layout from the times. ridegraph/cch.py implements Customizable CH (Dibbelt, Strasser and Wagner, 2016):

    1. Once per map: order junctions by nested dissection (cut the city along the direction that crosses the fewest roads, recurse on each half, rank the separator last), then contract in that order adding every shortcut, with no witness searches. On London: 77,124 roads become 301,179 edges and 3.19 million lower triangles, in 8 s.
    2. Whenever times change: load the current time of every road and sweep the triangles bottom-up, u->w = min(u->w, u->v + v->w). That takes 50 ms in numpy and about 20 ms in the browser.
    3. Queries: the usual two upward searches, with shortcuts unpacked through the triangles.

    The times come from Transport for London's open disruption feed (ridegraph/live.py). Each disrupted stretch is map-matched onto the graph (both ends and the middle of an edge within 18 m of the line). Closed roads get an infinite time, and open ones are slowed by severity. The map asks TfL directly when you switch on Live roadworks and closures. If the feed can't be reached from the browser, it falls back to a snapshot taken when CI built the page.

    python -m ridegraph live                      # what is disrupted right now
python -m ridegraph route 51.5130,-0.1590 51.5160,-0.1300 --at 13:00 --live

    6. Alternative routes and the Congestion Charge¶

    Map apps offer two or three routes because the quickest is not always the one you want. ridegraph/alternatives.py finds them with the penalty method: take the quickest route, make every road on it 40% slower, search again, and repeat. Each search is pushed off the corridor already used. A candidate is kept only if it is

    • at most 30% slower than the quickest (on real times, not penalised ones),
    • at most 65% shared with any route already offered, and
    • free of pointless detours: sampled stretches of a quarter of the trip must each be within 15% of the quickest way between their ends.

    Closing roads is the same machinery with infinite penalties, which is how a route avoids the Congestion Charge zone (ridegraph/charges.py: the zone polygon, £18 a day, 07:00 to 18:00 on weekdays and 12:00 to 18:00 at weekends; the boundary roads are free). From Paddington to Aldgate at 8am:

    Route Time Charge
    Quickest 36 min 32 s £18
    Alternative 1 40 min 11 s £18
    Alternative 2 45 min 27 s £18
    Avoiding the zone, along Euston Road and City Road 50 min 08 s none

    So staying out of the zone costs 14 minutes on this trip, about £1.30 a minute saved. The browser runs the same method (A* on the penalised weights, under 100 ms on London) and a Node test holds it to Python.

    python -m ridegraph route 51.5265,-0.1820 51.5100,-0.0700 --at 08:00 --alternatives --avoid-charge

    7. The learned ETA¶

    The router's estimate is only as good as its model of road speeds. It cannot know that lights on a particular corridor hold you for half a minute at 8:30, that one junction always queues, or that the evening peak arrives later than the timetable. Production ride-hailing systems therefore keep the routing engine and learn the residual: given a route and a moment, how far off will the engine be? ridegraph/eta.py follows that design.

    Features are things a production system knows when the trip is requested: the router's ETA, route length, straight-line distance and detour, the share of the route on main roads, B roads and side streets, the number of left, right, sharp and U-turns and of traffic lights on the route, time of day (as a point on a circle, so 23:59 sits next to 00:00), weekday or weekend, and where the route starts, ends and passes through.

    The target is log(observed time / router ETA), so the model learns a multiplicative correction and large and small trips weigh evenly.

    Observed trips. Real trip logs for London are not public, so the pipeline generates them, and it is open about it. A World is a hidden ground truth that differs from the router the way a real city would: congestion hotspots around a few busy junctions, a rush hour that peaks later and harder than the router assumes, main roads that clog more than modelled, waits at right turns and traffic lights that grow with traffic, occasional incidents and day-to-day noise. 20,000 trips between random points at random times are routed with Contraction Hierarchies and timed in that world. Nothing in the features reads the world directly; the models have to infer its effects from routes and outcomes.

    Results are measured on 4,000 trips the models never saw:

    Model Mean abs. error 90th pct. error Within 10%
    Router alone 27.5% 38.5% 2%
    Router times one global factor 10.1% 20.3% 58%
    Ridge regression 7.6% 15.5% 74%
    Neural network (MLP, 64x64) 7.3% 14.4% 75%
    Gradient-boosted trees (shipped to the map) 6.8% 13.8% 78%
    Graph neural network over every road 6.6% 13.4% 80%

    The simulated city is calibrated to central London: trips average about 21 km/h at night, 14 at midday and 11 at the peaks. The router alone is optimistic by about 28%, mostly because it knows nothing about traffic lights or about when the peaks really arrive. Time of day is the model's most useful signal, followed by where the route runs, weekday or weekend, length and the number of sets of lights. One global factor helps, a linear model helps more, and both non-linear models capture the interactions (lights cost more at peak; hotspots matter only when the route passes through them). Gradient-boosted trees win among the models that read one row per trip and, being a few hundred small trees, are also the easiest to ship.

    A graph neural network (ridegraph/gnn.py) works on the roads instead of on trip summaries, the approach DeepMind took for Google Maps. Every road segment starts with its own features (class, length, lights, junction size, position as sines and cosines), which are then averaged over the line graph (segments that lead into one another) for 1, 2, 4 and 8 hops. That is SIGN-style message passing, precomputed so it scales to all 127,000 segments. A two-layer network reads each segment's representation and the time of day and predicts its slowdown; a trip's time is the sum of router seconds x slowdown over its segments plus learned waits for each turn and set of lights. It is written in numpy with hand-derived gradients (checked against finite differences in the tests) and trained with Adam.

    On held-out London trips it edges out the trees: 6.6% error against 6.8%, 80% of trips within 10% against 78%. The trees still run in the browser, since the network needs the representation of every road (about 60 MB). The network's per-road slowdowns at 08:30 correlate at r = 0.60 with the hidden truth road by road, a harder test than the trees' area map below.

    Traffic lights in OpenStreetMap are often tagged on each approach to a junction and on nearby crossings, about 10 points per km of central London route. Signal nodes within 60 m along a route therefore count as one set (about 4 per km), both in the simulation and in the model's features.

    Arrival windows. A single number hides how much trips vary, so two more gradient-boosted models predict the 10th and 90th percentiles, and each trip gets a range such as "usually 25 to 36 min". Quantile models alone tend to be overconfident (77% coverage on London against an 80% target), so the window is calibrated with conformalized quantile regression: part of the training set is held back, the models' misses on it are measured, and the window is widened by exactly the margin that gives 80% coverage on new trips. On held-out London trips it lands at 81.0%, with misses split evenly above and below.

    What the model learned. The hidden hotspots are never shown to the model, yet it should find them, since trips through them run slow. The map's Learned slowdown layer asks the model how far off the router a typical trip through each grid square would be at the chosen time, and shades the square accordingly. Against the hidden hotspot field this map correlates at r = 0.83 on London (0.91 on a synthetic grid): the model has located the congestion from trip outcomes alone.

    How realistic is the simulated city? python -m ridegraph validate times random weekday trips (about 6 km) in it and sets the speeds beside published figures:

    Weekday time Simulated trips Router's estimate
    03:00 20.9 km/h 25.4 km/h
    08:00 11.1 km/h 16.2 km/h
    13:00 13.7 km/h 19.6 km/h
    17:30 10.9 km/h 16.8 km/h
    Published figure Source Speed
    City of London, local A roads DfT 2022 (CGN0503d) 12.4 km/h
    Camden, local A roads DfT 2022 (CGN0503d) 13.0 km/h
    London city centre, all day TomTom Traffic Index 2023 16.1 km/h
    London city centre, peak hours TomTom Traffic Index 2023 13.7 km/h

    Midday in the simulated city (13.7 km/h) sits right among the official figures for the boroughs it covers, and the router's own daytime estimate (16 to 20 km/h) is close to TomTom's all-day figure, which is what a router that knows nothing about lights or queues should look like. The simulated rush hours run about 20% slower than TomTom's peak figure, so if anything the models are trained and tested on a harsher London than the real one. The definitions differ (DfT counts A roads only, TomTom whole trips in its own city-centre area), so these are a range to sit within, not a target to fit.

    In the browser. The trees export to JSON, and web/js/eta.js computes the same features and walks the same trees. A test runs it under Node and requires its features and predictions to match Python's to nine decimal places.

    8. Travel-time maps and delivery rounds¶

    Travel-time maps (ridegraph/reach.py) answer "where can I get to in 20 minutes from here?", or, for someone choosing where to live, "from where can I get to my office in 20 minutes?". It is a single-source search that settles junctions in time order and stops at the budget, run on the reverse graph for journeys into a point. Reachable area is counted over 250 m squares. From Holborn, 10 minutes reaches 12.2 km² at 08:00 against 36.6 km² at 03:00: rush hour shrinks a 10-minute radius to a third.

    Delivery rounds (ridegraph/tour.py) find the best order to visit a list of stops, the travelling salesman problem on one-way streets, where A to B and B to A take different times:

    1. One many-to-many Contraction Hierarchies query prices every stop against every other at the chosen time of day.
    2. Held-Karp dynamic programming finds the provably best order, instant up to about a dozen stops.
    3. Nearest-next-stop construction plus 2-opt and Or-opt improvement give a fast heuristic for comparison and for larger jobs, re-costing whole tours on each move because reversing a stretch of an asymmetric tour changes its cost.
    Eight drops from Holborn, midday, round trip Time
    In the order entered 2 h 07 min
    Nearest next stop 1 h 20 min
    Nearest next stop + 2-opt/Or-opt 1 h 05 min
    Optimal (Held-Karp) 1 h 05 min 
    python -m ridegraph reach 51.5174,-0.1200 --at 08:00
python -m ridegraph tour 51.5174,-0.1200 51.5308,-0.1238 51.5033,-0.1145 ...

    9. Ride dispatch and demand forecasting¶

    Routing exists to answer a business question: which driver should take this ride? ridegraph/dispatch.py simulates a fleet over the 07:00 to 10:00 morning peak and compares six ways of answering it, on exactly the same ride requests and the same starting fleet.

    Demand (ridegraph/demand.py) comes from places with their own rhythm: main-line stations busy at commuting hours, the City and Holborn filling in the morning and emptying in the evening, Soho, Covent Garden and Shoreditch at night, plus requests from anywhere. Every day has its own level, its own surprises at each hotspot, and a couple of unannounced events (a match, a concert) that make one area surge for an hour or two.

    Pricing every pairing at once. Every 30 seconds the dispatcher needs the pickup time from every free driver to every waiting rider. Running one query per pair would be hundreds of searches; bucket-based many-to-many Contraction Hierarchies do it with one small upward search per driver and per rider, then join them through buckets at the nodes they share.

    The methods compared:

    • Nearest driver. Riders, oldest first, each take the closest free driver.
    • Optimal matching. The Hungarian algorithm (ridegraph/matching.py) assigns the whole batch at once to minimise total pickup time, with each rider's cost reduced by how long they have already waited so that hard-to-reach riders are not left until they give up.
    • Optimal + repositioning. Idle drivers also move towards neighbourhoods where expected demand over the next 15 minutes most exceeds the drivers already there, using either the historical average for that place and time, or an ML demand forecast.
    • + shared rides. The forecast method, and riders going the same way share a car. A waiting rider can join a car already carrying one rider: the car's position is the last junction it has reached on its route (every prefix of a quickest route is itself quickest, so a binary search over CH queries finds it), and every pickup and drop order is priced. Riders in the same batch can also pair up from the start. Nobody is held up by more than 40% of their own trip or six minutes, and the pairs that save most driving go first.
    • + surge pricing. The forecast method, and every two minutes each neighbourhood gets a price multiplier from how far waiting and expected riders outnumber nearby free drivers. Each rider accepts with probability exp(-(multiplier - 1)), and idle drivers are drawn towards surging areas. Surge here does not bring extra drivers online, so the comparison isolates its rationing effect.

    The forecast is a gradient-boosted Poisson model predicting ride requests per 500 m square for the next 15 minutes, from the time, the place and what has happened so far today (the last 15 minutes and the last hour, nearby and city-wide). On days it never saw:

    Forecast Poisson deviance (lower is better)
    Same as the last 15 minutes 3.35
    Historical average for the time 0.77
    Gradient-boosted forecast 0.39

    Results, averaged over 6 simulated London mornings with 115 drivers:

    Method Avg. wait 90th pct. wait Pickup drive Gave up Empty driving per driver
    Nearest driver 7.3 min 13.3 min 5.8 min 5.9% 0
    Optimal matching 6.6 min 12.1 min 5.4 min 6.2% 0
    Optimal + historical-average repositioning 6.2 min 11.6 min 5.1 min 4.9% 16 min
    Optimal + ML-forecast repositioning 6.1 min 11.3 min 4.9 min 5.0% 12 min
    + shared rides 5.1 min 9.6 min 4.4 min 1.4% 14 min
    + surge pricing 5.2 min 9.6 min 4.7 min 0.3% 14 min

    Optimal matching alone cuts waits by about 10%, and repositioning ahead of demand brings the total to 16%. The ML forecast halves the forecasting error of the historical average, but in this dispatch loop that shows up mainly as efficiency: it reaches the best waits while sending drivers on about a quarter less empty repositioning, because it moves them only where demand is actually building today. Better forecasts do not automatically mean proportionally better decisions; the repositioning rule is where the next gains would come from.

    The two product levers pull in opposite directions:

    On top of the ML-forecast method Riders served Shared Priced out Fleet driving per ride served
    Neither 95.0% none none 25.7 min
    Shared rides 98.6% 46% of rides none 22.1 min
    Surge pricing 91.3% none 8.5% 25.8 min

    Shared rides serve more people with less driving: 46% of rides are shared, waits fall by 16%, those who give up drop from 5.0% to 1.4%, and the fleet drives 14% less per rider served. The price is 2.1 extra minutes in the car for a shared rider. Surge pricing also shortens waits (5.2 min, almost nobody gives up), but by turning riders away: 8.5% decline the price, so fewer riders are served than with no surge at all. Without the extra drivers a higher price draws in, surge only rations a fixed fleet. That is the honest reading of the simulation, and the reason real platforms justify surge by its effect on supply.

    10. The live map¶

    The demo in web/ runs entirely in the browser, with no server:

    • Same data, same answers. python -m ridegraph export-web writes the graph and one Contraction Hierarchy per time-of-day preset as compact, gzipped binary arrays. web/js/engine.js is a direct port of the algorithms, and a test runs it under Node and requires exact agreement with the Python router on every preset.
    • The map is the graph. Rather than a tile server's picture of London, the page draws the very network being searched, styled like a street atlas: roads coloured by class, the Thames, and a lettered grid so routes read as "5C to 9I".
    • Search you can see. Every settled junction lights up its streets in the colour of the side that reached it, so Dijkstra visibly floods the city while Contraction Hierarchies touches a few hundred junctions scattered up the road hierarchy. All five methods (Customizable CH included) run on every trip, with junctions examined and query time side by side.
    • Routes, charges and closures. Each trip lists the quickest route and up to two alternatives with their times and whether they enter the Congestion Charge zone, which is drawn on the map; a switch adds the quickest route that stays out. Live roadworks and closures asks TfL's feed directly, draws the closed streets and re-customises the hierarchy, while plain CH steps aside because its shortcuts are stale.
    • Turn costs on demand. A switch charges for turns and traffic lights. The browser rebuilds the edge-expanded turn graph from per-manoeuvre turn kinds in the export, numbered exactly as the Python one, and the trip summary lists the right and left turns, U-turns and lights on the route. Contraction Hierarchies are switched off in this mode: an edge-based hierarchy for London would add around 20 MB per time of day, so turn-aware hierarchies live in the Python tool instead.
    • Learned ETA beside the router's. Every trip shows both estimates and a calibrated arrival window; a model card gives each model's error on held-out trips and the features the model leans on, and a map layer shades the city by the slowdown the model learned for the chosen time.
    • Four modes. Plan a route, Travel-time map (click anywhere; leaving or arriving; each band compared with 3am), Deliveries (click to add up to 12 stops; every ordering method compared, the best drawn and numbered) and Dispatch rides. The browser runs the same algorithms as Python, held to them by Node tests.
    • Dispatch you can watch. A Dispatch rides mode replays a simulated morning: cars coloured by what they are doing (orange when carrying two riders) move along their real routes, riders wait and are collected, surge squares show their multipliers, and every method's running averages update side by side. Only the decisions are stored; the browser rebuilds every route with the same hierarchy, and a test holds each leg to the duration the simulation recorded.
    • Built and deployed by CI. On each push, a workflow downloads central London from OpenStreetMap (cached between runs), snapshots TfL's disruptions, builds the graph, contracts it for each preset, trains the ETA models, simulates dispatch and publishes web/ to GitHub Pages.

    11. An HTTP API¶

    The map needs no server, but a product would. ridegraph/api.py serves the engine as a FastAPI application with OpenAPI docs at /docs:

    Endpoint Answers
    GET /route?from=lat,lon&to=lat,lon&at=08:15 The quickest route as GeoJSON, with distance, Congestion Charge and the learned ETA with its arrival window; alternatives=2 and avoid_charge=true add more routes
    GET /reach?from=lat,lon&minutes=10,20,30&arriving=false Area reachable within each time
    POST /tour Best visiting order for up to 13 stops, every method compared
    GET /health What is loaded

    Any minute of the day. The browser offers four preset times because each plain hierarchy is built for one set of travel times. The API answers for any time by keeping one Customizable CH and loading each quarter-hour's times into it on first use (tens of milliseconds), with recent quarter-hours cached, including the plain-list views the search loop reads.

    Measured with scripts/loadtest.py on central London, one worker, on a 2-vCPU machine that also ran the load generator: 14 ms median and 21 ms 95th-percentile latency for a route with its learned ETA, about 70 requests a second per worker, no errors over 2,000 random requests spread over six quarter-hours. Start-up, including building the hierarchy, takes 9 s. More throughput is more workers.

    pip install -e ".[api,ml]"
python -m ridegraph serve --eta-model web/data/eta-model.json
curl "http://127.0.0.1:8000/route?from=51.5154,-0.1410&to=51.5033,-0.1145&at=08:15"

docker build -t ridegraph . && docker run -p 8000:8000 ridegraph

    A CI workflow builds the image and checks the running container answers.

    12. What the London numbers show¶

    • Contraction Hierarchies work because real roads are hierarchical. On a uniform synthetic grid the reduction in search work is 24x; on London it is 389x, with preprocessing adding fewer shortcuts (121k) than the map has road segments. Main roads naturally end up at the top of the ranking.
    • A* barely helps in a city. Its estimate assumes the fastest road speed in a straight line, but London's average route speed is about half of that, so the search still covers a wide ellipse. This is why production engines rely on preprocessing rather than on geometric heuristics.
    • Bidirectional search halves the work in theory but not the time in Python, because juggling two frontiers costs interpreter overhead that eats most of the saving.

    13. Measuring fairly¶

    Benchmarks report nodes settled alongside wall-clock time. Settled nodes measure the work each algorithm does, independent of language; Python timings mostly measure interpreter overhead per node, which is why Contraction Hierarchies' 24x reduction in work shows up as a smaller time gain here. Every benchmark query is also cross-checked against Dijkstra, so a benchmark run doubles as a correctness check.

    Against OSRM. Agreeing with Dijkstra proves the searches are right, but they all share this package's map parsing and speed model. OSRM shares neither: it parses OpenStreetMap with its own C++ code and routes with Multi-Level Dijkstra. The Compare with OSRM workflow (ridegraph/osrm.py) gives OSRM v5.27 the ways this package treats as drivable, loads ridegraph's free-flow speed for every one of the 127,286 road segments as a segment speed file, switches off OSRM's turn and traffic-light penalties (the free-flow router has none) and asks both engines the same 1,000 random trips:

    ridegraph vs OSRM, 1,000 random central London trips
    Travel time within 1% of OSRM's 83.7%
    Travel time within 5% of OSRM's 96.9%
    Median time difference 0.23%
    Same route (95%+ of junctions shared) 75.0%
    Median share of junctions in common 99.7%
    Median query, ridegraph CH in Python 1.02 ms
    Median query, OSRM over local HTTP 1.56 ms

    What remains comes from the parts that are genuinely independent. OSRM snaps a coordinate to the nearest point on a road rather than to a junction, stores each segment's time in tenths of a second, and applies its own rules about which ways a car may use. Where the routes differ but the times agree, the two engines have found different routes that are equally quick. The query times are not a like-for-like contest (OSRM's include an HTTP round trip), but they show the Python hierarchy is in the same league as a production C++ engine on a city of this size.
