    Road rules and driving behaviour¶

    A route is only useful if a driver can legally follow it, and an arrival time is only useful if it reflects how people actually drive. This page sets out the rules and behaviours that matter for a ride-hailing or delivery router in a city like London, what Ridegraph models today, what it does not model yet, and how each would fit into the engine.

    Two kinds of rule are kept apart throughout. Legal rules decide whether a route is allowed at all, so they belong in the road graph: a banned turn is removed, not made expensive. Driving behaviour decides how long a legal route takes or how pleasant it is, so it belongs in the costs: a right turn across traffic is allowed, but it takes longer.

    Legal rules¶

    Turn restrictions¶

    Signs such as "No right turn", "No U-turn" and "Only straight on" are recorded in OpenStreetMap as restriction relations: a road you arrive on, the junction (or short stretch of road) you pass through, and a road you may not, or must, leave by. Central London has thousands of them.

    Ridegraph does not read these yet, which means some of its routes may use a turn that a driver is not allowed to make. This is the most important gap on this page, because it affects whether a route is correct rather than how long it takes.

    They fit naturally into the turn-aware graph Ridegraph already builds for turn costs (turns.py). In that graph every road segment is a node and every possible move from one segment to the next is an edge, so a "no right turn" is simply an edge that is never created, and an "only straight on" removes every other edge leaving that segment. Restrictions that span several junctions ("via ways") need the same idea applied to short chains of segments. A plain junction-to-junction graph cannot express a turn ban at all, which is one more reason the turn-aware graph is the right one for real routing.

    Bus lanes and bus gates¶

    Many London streets have lanes, or whole short sections, reserved for buses during some or all of the day. London's licensed black cabs may use most bus lanes; private hire cars, the category Uber drivers fall into, may not. The same journey can therefore have different quickest routes, and different times, depending on the vehicle.

    The clean way to model this is with vehicle profiles: one road graph, and a small table saying which roads and lanes each vehicle type may use and when. OpenStreetMap tags bus lanes and bus-only roads (busway, bus:lanes, access=no with psv=yes), so a taxi profile and a private hire profile could be compared on the same trip.

    Time-limited restrictions¶

    Some rules depend on the clock. School streets close to traffic around the start and end of the school day, some low-traffic neighbourhoods are filtered only at certain times, and "no entry except for access" roads are legal only if your trip starts or ends on them.

    Ridegraph already prices travel by time of day, and its Customizable Contraction Hierarchies can load a new set of travel times in about 20 ms, so a time-limited closure can be applied in the same way as a live closure: set the affected roads to impassable for the hours the rule applies and re-customise. Access-only roads need a different treatment, allowing them only as the first or last part of a route.

    Charges and zones¶

    The Congestion Charge is modelled: routes can be priced, and a route that stays out of the zone can be requested. The Ultra Low Emission Zone (ULEZ) now covers the whole of Greater London and charges older, more polluting vehicles each day they drive in it. Most ride-hailing cars meet its standard, so it is better treated as a property of the vehicle profile than of the route: a non-compliant vehicle pays it on any London trip.

    Speed limits¶

    Much of central London now has a 20 mph limit: the City of London, boroughs such as Camden and Islington, and the main TfL roads within the Congestion Charge zone. Ridegraph reads the posted limit from OpenStreetMap where it is tagged and assumes traffic flows somewhat below it; where a road has no limit tagged, it falls back on a typical speed for that class of road. The estimate is only as good as the tagging, so the fallback matters most on minor streets.

    Where a car may stop¶

    A pickup point is only useful if a driver may legally stop there. Red routes (major roads marked with red lines and managed by TfL) restrict stopping, as do double red lines, bus stops and zig-zag markings near crossings. A good pickup point is a short walk from the rider on a street where stopping is allowed, not the exact point they tapped.

    Ridegraph currently snaps a pickup to the nearest junction. Choosing legal stopping points needs kerb data that OpenStreetMap only partly records, so this is the hardest item here to do well.

    Driving behaviour (cost, not law)¶

    Turning across traffic¶

    Turning across oncoming traffic, right in the UK and left in most of the world, means waiting for a gap. It is slower, more likely to cause a collision and wastes fuel while idling. UPS is well known for planning its delivery routes to avoid these turns where it can.

    Ridegraph's turn costs already reflect this: in the UK a right turn costs more than a left, a sharp turn more again, and the costs swap sides for cities that drive on the right (Manhattan). A delivery-van profile could go further and make right turns so expensive that they are only used when there is no reasonable alternative.

    Kerbside pickup¶

    When a rider is waiting on one side of a busy road, a car that arrives on the other side forces them to cross, or forces the driver into a U-turn. Uber and other ride-hailing apps try to send the driver to the rider's side of the road. In routing research this is known as the curb approach: a stop is not just a place but a place and a side, and the vehicle must arrive travelling in the direction that puts the kerb next to it.

    To model it, a pickup becomes a point on a directed road segment rather than a junction. In the UK the rider should be on the car's left, so only one direction of travel along that segment is acceptable. The search then has to reach that segment from the right end, which can add a minute or two to the pickup but saves the rider a crossing. The dispatch simulator could then report how much extra driving kerbside pickups cost the fleet, and what it saves riders.

    Waiting at traffic lights¶

    Ridegraph adds the same average wait, 12 seconds, at every set of traffic lights. Real waits depend on the signal's cycle length, how much of the cycle is green for your direction, and how busy the junction is. Webster's formula, a classic result from traffic engineering, estimates the average delay from exactly those three things, and rises steeply as a junction approaches capacity.

    Signal timings are not public for most junctions, but the formula would still let the wait grow at peak times and on busier roads instead of staying flat. The learned arrival-time model already corrects part of this from data: the number of traffic lights on a route is one of its most useful features.

    Time spent at each stop¶

    Every pickup and drop-off takes time: pulling in, the rider getting in or out, luggage, payment. For a single ride it is small; for a delivery round with a dozen stops, or a shared ride that picks up twice, it adds up. A fixed allowance of one to two minutes per stop is a common starting point. Neither the dispatch simulator nor the delivery planner includes it yet: pickups and drop-offs are instant, so their totals are driving time only.

    Order of work¶

    1. Turn restrictions, because without them a route can be illegal.
    2. Kerbside pickup, because it is the most distinctive ride-hailing problem here and changes what the dispatch results mean.
    3. Vehicle profiles (taxi, private hire, delivery van), which bring bus lanes, ULEZ and stronger turn avoidance in together.
    4. Time-limited restrictions, using the same mechanism as live closures.
    5. Signal delays and stop times, which refine the arrival-time estimates.
